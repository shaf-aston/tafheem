# Build on public-domain DLI, not Damascene datasets

No openly licensed source ready for commercial use combines Damascene dialect, Arabic script, transliteration, English, and situational dialogues with replies. The best reusable backbone is the **US Defense Language Institute (DLI) Syrian Arabic course**. As a federal work it is presumptively public domain. It is Syrian-specific, runs to at least 9 modules with audio, and opens with situation units such as greetings, meeting people, and visiting. Layer small, commercially safe supplements on top of it. **Tatoeba** North Levantine sentences are CC BY 2.0 FR. **Wikimedia** phrasebooks, Wiktionary lemmas and Wikipedia grammar articles are CC BY-SA and can supply MSA-vs-colloquial notes. **Peace Corps Jordan** lessons can supply culture notes, adapted to Damascene. The richest datasets by content fit are Levanti (English plus transliteration), MADAR (city-level Damascus) and UFAL North Levantine, but all three are **non-commercial**, so avoid them unless you negotiate a license. So will your team be writing the dialogues? Yes, mostly. The open sources supply raw material, vocabulary, and dated but legal dialogue scaffolding, not finished lessons. Many licenses and page contents were read only from search snippets because the research environment blocked archive.org, Hugging Face, Wikimedia and others. Every "safe" rating below still needs a manual check on the live page before you ship.

## DLI Syrian is the only open source shaped like a course

The DLI course matters because it is already organized the way your app is. Module 1 covers **Greeting People, Meeting People, Inviting and Visiting People, and A Visit to an Office** ([Live Lingua](https://www.livelingua.com/course/dli/syrian_arabic_course_-_module_1)). Live Lingua has separate pages up to Module 9, so the course has at least nine modules ([Live Lingua Module 9](https://livelingua.com/arabic/courses/dli/Syrian_Arabic_Course_-_Module_9)). There is also a "General Material" set of six supplementary books, including an Arabic–English glossary and a sounds-and-script book ([Live Lingua](https://www.livelingua.com/course/dli/syrian_arabic_language_course_-_general_material)). Work by US federal employees is not copyrightable, and aggregators host the course as free. That makes it the one substantial Syrian-dialect source you can adapt commercially without share-alike obligations. There are three costs. The 438 MB size suggests image scans, so Arabic script will need OCR and hand correction. The romanization is dated. And the course's precise dialect and date, and whether it includes Arabic script at all, were not confirmed. Download from archive.org or the Yojik mirror ([Mod 01.pdf](https://fsi-languages.yojik.eu/languages/DLI/Arabic-Syrian/Books/Mod%2001.pdf)) and cite that as the provenance, rather than relying on Live Lingua, whose own terms went unread.

Peace Corps Jordan is the closest match for **culture notes and learner-friendly layout**. Its 10 lessons cover greetings, family, food and communication difficulties, and give Arabic, transliteration and English together ([Peace Corps](https://www.peacecorps.gov/educators-and-students/educators/resources/arabic-jordan/)). Live Lingua describes Peace Corps courses as public domain ([Live Lingua](https://www.livelingua.com/peace-corps/)). However, Peace Corps material is sometimes contractor-authored, so check the PDF's title page. The forms are Jordanian (South Levantine), so each phrase needs a Damascene rewrite. Search found no Peace Corps Syria or Lebanon course, and no FSI "Spoken Syrian" course.

## Permissive supplements carry obligations, not just content

Tatoeba is the cleanest license in the set. Its sentences are **CC BY 2.0 FR**, so commercial reuse needs only attribution, and bulk TSV exports link North Levantine sentences to English ([Tatoeba downloads](https://tatoeba.org/en/downloads)). The catch is quality and script. Many entries are Latin-script chat-style romanizations such as "El-bent 7elwe ktir." ([Tatoeba](https://tatoeba.org/eng/sentences/show/3076284)). They are not tagged as Damascene, and the exact counts were not retrieved.

Wikimedia content is useful for **MSA-vs-colloquial contrast notes** and vocabulary lookup. Wiktionary splits North and South Levantine lemmas by part of speech ([Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Category:North_Levantine_Arabic_lemmas)). Wikibooks' beginner course marks North and South variants with (N) and (S) ([Wikibooks](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Levantine_Arabic)). Wikipedia's grammar and vocabulary articles document the contrasts in prose ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levantine_Arabic_vocabulary)). There is no Syrian or Levantine Wikivoyage phrasebook, only Jordanian, Lebanese and a generic MSA-leaning Arabic one ([Wikivoyage](https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Arabic_phrasebook)). Phrasebook entries generally have no replies or dialogues. CC BY-SA requires you to release adapted text under the same license. Keep Wikimedia-derived phrases in a **separately licensed content pack** with attribution and change notes, so the share-alike obligation does not spread to your proprietary lessons or code.

## The best-fitting data is fenced off by non-commercial licenses

The datasets closest to your needs are all unusable commercially as published. **Levanti** has about 500K sentences with English, diacritics and transliteration, including Syrian. But only **42K are real and human-validated**; the rest are synthetic, generated by an LLM. Its related models are CC BY-NC 4.0, which suggests the dataset is non-commercial too ([HF model](https://huggingface.co/guymorlan/levanti_diacritics2translit)). **MADAR** is the only city-level Damascus/Aleppo travel corpus. It is non-commercial, and its English side "is not provided because of copyright restrictions" and must be requested from the U-Star Consortium ([MADAR](https://camel.abudhabi.nyu.edu/madar-parallel-corpus/)). **UFAL North Levantine** has 120,600 conversational subtitle sentences with English, under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 ([LINDAT](https://lindat.mff.cuni.cz/repository/xmlui/handle/11234/1-5033)). The remaining corpora fall short on other grounds. MPCA has Syrian plus English but is available only on request ([ACL](https://aclanthology.org/L14-1435/)). PADIC and Shami have no English. LDC Levantine is paid and mostly Lebanese ([LDC](https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/LDC2007S01)).

Several popular resources look free but are copyrighted, and none should be ingested. *Syrian Colloquial Arabic: A Functional Course* by Liddicoat and colleagues is a commercial book; only its sample chapter is free, even though full copies circulate on archive.org ([syrianarabic.com](https://www.syrianarabic.com/)). *Shou fi ma fi?* appears on OER Commons, but the book is All Rights Reserved ([OER Commons](https://oercommons.org/authoring/3886-shou-fi-ma-fi-intermediate-levantine-arabic-review/view)). The list continues with Lingualism, Talk In Arabic, Arabic Unlocked, Pimsleur, [learnlevantine.com](https://learnlevantine.com/), [Levantine Online](https://www.levantineonline.com/levantine-dialect) and Cowell's reference grammar. OpenArabic.org is a directory of other courses, not a content source in its own right ([OpenArabic](https://openarabic.org/syrian-arabic-resources/)). Khallina.org describes itself as "open source" and has Arab-culture modules, but its license is unconfirmed ([Khallina](https://khallina.org/)).

## What must be verified before shipping

Because much of the research ran on search snippets, a manual pass with open network access has to confirm the following. For the DLI books, check for contractor or third-party copyright notices, the publication year, the module count, whether they use Arabic script or transliteration, whether the dialect is Damascene, and whether the files are text or scans. Read the author and copyright page of the Peace Corps Jordan PDF. Pull the actual counts of Tatoeba apc/ajp sentences linked to English. Check the real section structure of the Wikivoyage phrasebooks and whether they include Arabic script. Read the dataset-card licenses for Levanti, ArSyra and AraDiCE, and the terms for PADIC and MPCA. Check Khallina's license. Finally, if a non-commercial source is worth licensing, write to Levanti's author and to CAMeL and U-Star for MADAR. Search also found **no** Levantine course on MIT OCW, the Open Textbook Library or LibreTexts, but that was not an exhaustive catalog check.

## Conclusion

The open landscape inverts what you would want. The most modern, best-annotated Damascene and Levantine data is locked behind non-commercial terms, while the legally free material is decades old and Syrian only in a general sense. The practical result is that your lessons' Damascene authenticity will come from native-speaker authoring and review, not from any corpus. Use open sources as scaffolding and vocabulary, not as finished copy. That points to a two-track content architecture. The first track is proprietary, native-reviewed dialogues seeded from public-domain DLI situations. The second is a clearly separated CC BY / CC BY-SA attribution pack for Tatoeba and Wikimedia material. If a Damascene corpus with English is ever needed at scale, a paid license for MADAR or Levanti is probably cheaper than reconstructing it.
